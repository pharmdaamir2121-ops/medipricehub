Medicine Shortages: What Consumers Should Know

Health Guides · Updated 2026

From time to time, a particular medicine or brand becomes hard to find at pharmacies. This can be frustrating, especially for medicines taken regularly. Understanding why shortages happen — and what your practical options are — can help you respond calmly rather than panic-buying or turning to unverified sources.

Common causes of shortages

  • Raw material supply issues: Many active ingredients are imported, and disruptions in the global supply chain can delay local production.
  • Currency and import costs: Sharp currency fluctuations can make importing raw materials less viable for manufacturers, sometimes leading to temporary production pauses.
  • Regulatory or pricing changes: Adjustments to regulated prices can occasionally affect a manufacturer's willingness to continue producing a specific pack size in the short term.
  • Seasonal demand spikes: Certain medicines see sudden demand increases during flu season, dengue season, or public health events, which can outpace normal stock levels.
  • Distribution issues: A medicine may be in stock nationally but temporarily unavailable in a specific city or pharmacy due to distribution timing.

What you can do

  1. Ask your pharmacist about alternatives. Often a different brand with the same generic ingredient and strength is available and equally appropriate — but confirm this with a pharmacist or your doctor rather than assuming.
  2. Check nearby pharmacies before assuming a nationwide shortage. Availability can vary a lot by location.
  3. Avoid stockpiling more than you need. Panic-buying can worsen shortages for others who need the medicine too.
  4. Never buy from unverified or informal sellers just to avoid a wait. This significantly raises the risk of counterfeit or substandard products — see our guide on spotting counterfeit medicines.
  5. Contact your doctor if a shortage affects an ongoing treatment, especially for chronic conditions, so they can advise on suitable alternatives.
If a medicine you rely on regularly is affected by a shortage, don't stop or change your treatment on your own — speak with your doctor or pharmacist first.

Staying informed

DRAP and manufacturer announcements sometimes provide updates on supply issues for specific products. Checking with your pharmacist directly is usually the fastest way to get accurate, local information about availability.
