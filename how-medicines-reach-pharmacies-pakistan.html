How medicines reach your pharmacy: from company to distributor to shop

Last reviewed 18 November 2026

Short answer: Most medicines follow a simple path: the manufacturing company, then a distributor, then your pharmacy, then you. Each step matters for price and for safety. Heat during storage and transport can damage medicines, and discounts that companies give to the trade do not change the maximum retail price printed on your pack.

The usual path of a medicine

StepWhoWhat happens
1The companyThe manufacturer or importer makes or brings in the medicine and prints the batch number, dates and retail price on the pack.
2The distributorThe distributor buys from the company, stores the stock and supplies pharmacies, usually in smaller quantities and more often.
3The pharmacyThe pharmacy buys from the distributor, stores the medicine and sells it to you.
4YouYou buy at a price that should not be above the maximum retail price printed on the pack.

This is the common pattern in the trade. Some products reach pharmacies in other ways, but a licensed distributor in the middle is the usual route. A distributor should be licensed, and a pharmacy you trust should be able to tell you where its stock comes from.

A problem I often see: storage in hot weather

In my work in distribution, the problem I see most often is how medicines are stored in hot weather. Summers in Pakistan are hot, and medicines can be left in warehouses, delivery vehicles and shop shelves that become far hotter than the pack allows.

Many packs say "store in a cool, dry place" or give a temperature limit, and some medicines must be kept refrigerated. Heat can make some medicines lose strength before their expiry date, and the expiry date assumes the medicine was stored as the label says. This is why it matters where you buy.

What you can notice as a customer

  • Does the pharmacy keep medicines out of direct sunlight and away from heat?
  • Is the shop reasonably cool, and are refrigerated products kept in a fridge?
  • Do the tablets, capsules or syrups look normal, without damp, melting, stickiness or a strange colour or smell?
  • Is the pack in good condition, with the label and printed price clear?

At home, the same rules apply. Read how to store medicines safely at home.

Something customers rarely know: company discounts

Companies often give discounts to distributors and pharmacies. These are part of how the trade works. They can affect what a pharmacy pays for its stock, but they do not change the maximum retail price printed on the pack.

What this means for you:

  • The printed retail price is the most a pharmacy may charge. See how DRAP sets medicine prices.
  • A pharmacy may choose to sell for less than the printed price. Whether it does is the pharmacy's decision, so there is nothing wrong with asking politely whether a discount is available.
  • A cheaper price is not a reason to skip the safety checks. A genuine discount and a suspiciously low price are different things. See how to spot fake or unregistered medicines.
  • The price the pharmacy paid is called the trade price. You can read what the word means in medicine price terms explained.

Why the supply chain matters to you

  • Safety. Every extra, unlicensed step is a chance for bad storage, mix-ups or fake products to enter the chain.
  • Price. The printed maximum retail price is the same wherever the medicine was bought in the chain.
  • Trust. A pharmacy that can answer where its medicines come from, and keeps them properly, is easier to trust.

Questions you can ask your pharmacy

  • Where does this medicine come from, and which company made it?
  • How is this product stored in the shop?
  • Is the price the same as the price printed on the pack?
  • When does this batch expire?

Frequently asked questions

Can a pharmacy sell above the price printed on the pack?

It should not. The printed retail price is the maximum. If you are asked to pay more, politely ask why, and report it if it continues. See how DRAP sets medicine prices.

Why might one pharmacy sell for less than another?

A pharmacy can choose to sell below the printed price, for example when it receives discounts from companies or distributors. Compare the exact same product and pack, and check the safety points before you buy.

Does hot weather really damage medicines?

It can. Heat and humidity can affect some medicines, which is why labels give storage instructions. If a medicine looks or smells different from normal, ask your pharmacist before you use it.
