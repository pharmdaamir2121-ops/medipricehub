What to do if a pharmacy charges more than the printed price

Last reviewed 20 November 2026

Short answer: The retail price printed on a medicine pack is the most a seller should charge for it. If you are asked to pay more, check the pack, ask politely for an explanation, keep evidence, and report it to your provincial health department or drug control authority, or to DRAP, if the problem is not fixed.

Step 1: Check before you complain

Make sure you are comparing the right things, so you do not accuse a shop by mistake.

  • Same product and pack. A different strength, a different pack size or a different brand will have a different price. Check the name, strength and number of tablets or the volume.
  • Price per pack, not per tablet. If you are buying a single strip from a larger pack, the shop may be quoting a price per strip. Do the sum with our guide on pack sizes.
  • The printed price is clear. Look for the retail price on the carton or strip. If it is scratched out, covered by a sticker or looks handwritten, that is a warning sign. See how to read a medicine label.
  • The official price, if there is one. You can look up registered products on DRAP's PharmaPrice Index. See how DRAP sets medicine prices for what the printed price means.

Step 2: Ask politely

Most misunderstandings are cleared up with a calm question. You might say:

  • "The price printed on this pack is Rs [amount]. Can you tell me why the price today is higher?"
  • "Could you please show me the printed price on the pack again?"

Keep your voice calm. A shopkeeper may have misread the pack, quoted the wrong item or made a billing mistake. If it is an honest mistake, it can be fixed on the spot.

Step 3: Keep evidence

If the shop will not charge the printed price, collect what you need before you leave.

  • A clear photo of the pack, showing the name, strength, batch number, expiry date and the printed price.
  • The receipt or bill, with the price you were charged. If the shop will not give one, write down the price and the time.
  • The name and address of the pharmacy, and the date.

Do not argue loudly, and do not take or damage anything. Evidence is more useful than a heated conversation.

Step 4: Decide whether to buy

You do not have to buy from a shop that overcharges. For a medicine you need urgently, you may prefer to buy it and report afterwards. For a non-urgent medicine, you can try another licensed pharmacy. Keep in mind that a very low price is not a safe sign either. See how to spot fake or unregistered medicines.

Step 5: Report it

If you believe a pharmacy is selling above the maximum price, you can report it to:

  • your provincial health department or drug control authority, which oversees pharmacies in your province;
  • the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP), through its website at dra.gov.pk.

The exact process and contact details differ by province and can change, so check the official website or call the office for the current way to complain. Send your photos, receipt and the pharmacy's details with your report. We cannot take reports ourselves, and we do not investigate shops.

Why a price above the printed price should not be accepted

The price printed on the pack is the maximum retail price. A seller may charge less, but should not charge more. Pakistan's drug rules require the retail price to be printed on the label, and DRAP publishes price notifications and a product price list. This is why the printed price is the most reliable guide you have at the counter.

A shortage does not change this. When a medicine is hard to find, some sellers may ask for extra, but the maximum price printed on the pack is still the maximum.

Important: This guide gives general information only and is not legal advice. Rules and reporting processes can change, so check the official source. Never take or stop a medicine because of a price dispute without asking your doctor or pharmacist. See our Disclaimer.

Frequently asked questions

Can a pharmacy charge more than the printed price?

It should not. The printed price is the maximum. A pharmacy may charge less, for example when it offers a discount.

What if the printed price is covered or unreadable?

Ask the shop to explain, and check the product on DRAP's PharmaPrice Index. A covered or changed price can also be a sign of a problem with the pack, so be careful before you buy.

What if the medicine is in short supply?

A shortage does not allow a higher price than the one printed on the pack. Ask your pharmacist for a registered alternative, but do not switch a prescribed medicine without checking with your doctor.

Does reporting a shop really help?

It gives the authorities information they may act on. We cannot promise an outcome, so keep your evidence and follow up if you wish.
