How to read a prescription: common abbreviations explained

Last reviewed 23 October 2026

Short answer: Doctors often write prescriptions in shorthand, such as Tab for tablet, BD for twice a day and SOS for "if needed". This page explains the most common abbreviations so you can understand what is written. It is for understanding only. Always confirm how to take your medicine with your doctor or pharmacist.

Why prescriptions use abbreviations

Many of the abbreviations come from Latin and have been used by doctors for a long time. They save writing time, but they can confuse patients, and handwriting can make them harder to read. Do not try to guess. If you cannot read or understand something on a prescription, ask the pharmacist to explain it before you leave the shop.

What the medicine is: form and route

Written asMeaning
Rx"Take" or "prescription". It often appears at the top of the medicine list.
TabTablet
CapCapsule
Syp or SuspSyrup or suspension (a liquid medicine)
InjInjection
Oint or CreamOintment or cream, applied to the skin
DropsDrops, for the eye, ear or nose, or to take by mouth. Check which.
mg, g, mlMilligram, gram and millilitre, the units of strength and volume

A strength such as "500mg" is part of the product. A different strength is a different product, so make sure the pack matches what is written. See how to read a medicine label.

How often: frequency

Written asMeaning
ODOnce a day
BD or BIDTwice a day
TDS or TIDThree times a day
QIDFour times a day
HSAt bedtime
SOS or PRNOnly when needed, for example for pain. Ask how often and how many in a day you may take.
StatImmediately, as a single first dose

Take care with "OD". On eye medicines, "OD" can mean the right eye, so ask the pharmacist if you are unsure, especially with eye drops. Where a prescription gives a number of days, such as "x 5 days", that is how long to take the medicine.

When in relation to food

Written asMeaning
ACBefore meals
PCAfter meals
With food or after foodTake with or just after a meal, which can reduce stomach upset for some medicines
Empty stomachUsually means about an hour before or two hours after food. Ask the pharmacist to be precise.

Reading a sample line

Suppose a prescription says: Tab X 500mg, 1 BD, PC, x 5 days. In plain words it means: one tablet of X, 500mg strength, twice a day, after meals, for five days. This is only an example of how to read the shorthand. It is not a recommendation for any medicine. "X" is a made-up name used for illustration.

Check these things before you leave the pharmacy

  1. The names, strengths and forms on the pack match what is written on the prescription.
  2. You know how many times a day to take each medicine, and for how many days.
  3. You know whether to take it before or after food.
  4. You know what to do if you miss a dose, and what side effects to watch for.
  5. The expiry date is well ahead. See medicine expiry dates explained.

If you want to change a brand to save money

Do not change a brand, strength or form on your own. A pharmacist can often suggest a registered alternative with the same ingredient and strength, but for a prescribed medicine, check with your doctor first. Read brand vs generic medicines for what to compare.

Important: This guide explains shorthand only. It does not tell you what to take or how much. Follow your doctor's and pharmacist's instructions, and ask if anything is unclear. In an emergency, call your local emergency services. See our Disclaimer.

Frequently asked questions

What if I cannot read my doctor's handwriting?

Ask the pharmacist, and if they are not certain, ask them to check with the doctor. Do not guess the name or the dose of a medicine.

Does "SOS" mean I can take as much as I like?

No. It means "if needed", not "without limit". Ask your doctor or pharmacist how often you may take it and the most you can take in a day.

Can a pharmacist explain my prescription to me?

Yes. Explaining how to take a prescribed medicine is an important part of a pharmacist's work.
